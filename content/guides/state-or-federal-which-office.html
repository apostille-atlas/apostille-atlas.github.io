The rule is simple: whoever signed the document decides where it goes. The office that apostilles it must be able to verify that official's signature.

Goes to the state where the document was issued or notarized

  • Birth, death, marriage and divorce certificates (certified copies)
  • State and county court documents
  • Documents notarized by a notary commissioned in that state
  • School documents (usually notarized first; rules vary by state)

Goes to the U.S. Department of State

  • Documents signed by federal officials, such as FBI background checks (Identity History Summaries)
  • Other federal agency records

State offices say this directly. For example, Illinois says it cannot authenticate FBI background checks or federal documents, and Florida tells you to send federal documents to the U.S. Department of State.

Watch for states with an unusual issuing office

  • Georgia: apostilles come from the GSCCCA (the Superior Court Clerks' Cooperative Authority), not the Secretary of State.
  • New Jersey: handled by the Treasury's Division of Revenue and Enterprise Services, with an online order step first.
  • New York: many local records need County Clerk certification before the state will apostille them.
