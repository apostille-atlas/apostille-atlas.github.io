If you need a U.S. birth certificate, diploma, power of attorney or FBI background check accepted in mainland China, the process got much shorter in late 2023. China acceded to the Hague Apostille Convention on March 8, 2023, and the Convention entered into force for China on November 7, 2023 (HCCH). Since then, one U.S. apostille replaces the old chain of state certification, U.S. Department of State authentication and Chinese embassy legalization (Embassy of China in the U.S.).

Do U.S. documents for China still need embassy legalization?

No. The Embassy of China in the U.S. says that starting November 7, 2023, public documents within the scope of the Convention issued by the U.S. "only need to apply for a U.S. apostille" before they can be sent to mainland China, with no consular authentication by the U.S. or by the Chinese Embassy and Consulates-General. On the same date, the embassy and consulates stopped offering consular authentication (embassy notice). The Chinese Consulate General in New York repeats this and adds that once you have the apostille, "you can directly use it in Chinese Mainland" without applying for anything else at the consulate (Consulate in New York).

What about Hong Kong and Macao?

The Convention was already in force in the Hong Kong and Macao Special Administrative Regions before China joined, and it continues to apply there (HCCH, embassy notice). Some state request forms ask you to name which one: Michigan's form, for example, says to mark Mainland, Hong Kong or Macao when the destination is China (Michigan form).

Where do I get the apostille for a document going to China?

It depends on who signed the document, as for any Hague country (HCCH list of U.S. competent authorities):

  • Birth, marriage, divorce and death certificates issued by a county or state: the Los Angeles consulate says you can apostille the original certified copy directly, at the state's apostille office (Consulate in Los Angeles). See our birth certificate and marriage certificate guides.
  • Personal documents you sign, such as a power of attorney for a property sale or lawsuit, a declaration renouncing an inheritance, a no-spouse (single status) declaration or a same-person declaration: the New York and Los Angeles consulates say to have a local notary notarize it first, then take it to the state's apostille office (Consulate in New York). See our power of attorney guide.
  • Federal documents, such as an FBI criminal record check: the U.S. Department of State, not a state office (Consulate in Los Angeles). See below.

The New York consulate warns that each state processes apostilles differently (by mail, in person, online pre-check), so confirm the method on the state's own page first. How do I apostille an FBI background check for China?

The FBI is a federal agency, so the Los Angeles consulate says the apostille comes from the U.S. Department of State (Consulate in Los Angeles). The Department of State charges $20 per document with Form DS-4194, and processes mailed requests within 5 weeks of receipt, or walk-in drop-offs in Washington, DC within 7 business days (U.S. Department of State; details checked on our Department of State guide). The Chinese consulates' pages we checked do not say how recent the FBI check must be. That rule, if any, comes from the office in China that receives it (for example your employer or the work-permit authority), so ask them before you order the check.

Does a U.S. apostille guarantee China will accept my document?

No. The embassy notes that an apostille only confirms the signature, the signer's capacity and, where needed, the seal on the document. It "does not guarantee the acceptance of the public document" by the user in China, and recommends checking format, content, time limit and translation requirements with the receiving party in advance (embassy notice).

Does my document need a Chinese translation?

A U.S. apostille is issued in English. According to the Los Angeles consulate, if the receiving party in China requires a translation, you can have the apostille and the document translated into Chinese and the translation notarized at a notary office in China (Consulate in Los Angeles). No fee for that is published on the consulate's page. More on timing: apostille plus certified translation.

What about Chinese documents going to the U.S.?

That runs the other way: China's Ministry of Foreign Affairs is China's competent authority, and authorized provincial and municipal foreign affairs offices can issue apostilles for documents from their regions (Ministry of Foreign Affairs of China). This site covers U.S. offices only.
