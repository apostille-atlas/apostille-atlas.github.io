If you are moving to the Philippines, applying for a 13(a) visa as the spouse of a Filipino citizen, getting married there, or sending a U.S. power of attorney or school record to Manila, you may be told to get your document "red ribboned" at a Philippine consulate. That step no longer exists. The Philippines acceded to the Apostille Convention on September 12, 2018 and it entered into force there on May 14, 2019 (HCCH status table), so a U.S. apostille is the only authentication a U.S. public document needs.

Do U.S. documents need an apostille for the Philippines?

Yes, when the Philippine office asks for an authenticated U.S. public document, the apostille replaces the old consular authentication. The Philippine Consulate General in New York says that since May 15, 2019 it no longer authenticates documents notarized by a U.S. notary public or signed, issued or certified by a federal, state, county, city, university or school official; such documents "will only require an Apostille Certificate" and need no further diplomatic or consular legalization, so they can be sent to and used directly in the Philippines (Philippine Consulate General New York). The Los Angeles consulate says the same (Philippine Consulate General Los Angeles).

Which U.S. office apostilles my document for the Philippines?

The authority for the U.S. jurisdiction that issued or notarized it (HCCH; Philippine Consulate General Los Angeles):

  • State documents (birth, marriage, divorce and death certificates, state court records, school records, documents notarized by a state-commissioned notary): the apostille office of that state, generally the Secretary of State. For a birth certificate, that is the state where you were born; for a notarized document, the state that commissioned the notary. The New York consulate notes that the U.S. Department of State does not apostille state-issued documents (PCG New York). See the official fee, address and method on our pages for California, Texas, New York, New Jersey, Washington and the rest of the directory.
  • Federal documents (documents signed by a federal official, a U.S. consular officer or a military notary, and Consular Reports of Birth Abroad): the U.S. Department of State (PCG New York; PCG Los Angeles), which charges $20 per document (HCCH) and processes mailed requests within 5 weeks of receipt (U.S. Department of State). See our Department of State guide.
  • Federal court documents: the clerks and deputy clerks of the federal courts (PCG New York; HCCH).

Can the U.S. Embassy in Manila apostille my document?

No. The U.S. Embassy in the Philippines says it "cannot authenticate or apostille any U.S. civil documents"; they must be apostilled by the state that issued them or, for certain federal documents, by the Office of Authentications in Washington, D.C. If asked, the Embassy or the Consular Agency in Cebu can notarize your own sworn statement that documents you present are authentic, by appointment (U.S. Embassy in the Philippines). Whether a Philippine office accepts that affidavit is up to that office.

Which documents need an apostille for a 13(a) spouse visa?

At the Philippine Consulate General in Los Angeles, a 13(a) immigrant visa application by the spouse of a Filipino citizen must include the applicant's apostilled birth certificate and an apostilled local or state police clearance obtained within the last 6 months, among other documents; the visa fee is $150 by cashier's check (or U.S. Postal money order by mail). Unmarried children under 21 of a Filipino citizen need an apostilled birth certificate (PCG Los Angeles 13(a) visa). That page does not ask for an FBI background check; check the requirement page of the consulate that covers your state, since lists can differ. For the birth certificate, see our birth certificate apostille guide.

Does my U.S. birth certificate need an apostille to get married in the Philippines?

We found no national rule on this on the official pages we checked. What is published: the U.S. government cannot issue a certificate of legal capacity to marry, but the U.S. Embassy notarizes a U.S. citizen's Affidavit of Legal Capacity to Contract Marriage and offers templates for Manila and Cebu (U.S. Embassy notarials). Since April 2021, under Philippine Statistics Authority Memorandum Circular No. 2021-004, that affidavit may also be notarized locally in the Philippines, and the Embassy tells couples to check with the PSA or the local civil registry office where they plan to marry (U.S. Embassy message, April 6, 2021). If that office asks for an apostilled birth certificate or divorce decree, get it before you travel; see our divorce decree and marriage certificate guides.

Do I still need a consulate for a power of attorney?

You have two routes. A document you sign before a U.S. notary is apostilled by the state that commissioned the notary; the consulates will no longer authenticate it (PCG New York). Philippine consulates also still perform consular notarization for documents to be used in the Philippines, which is a separate service listed on their notarial pages (PCG Los Angeles); check the consulate's own requirements and fees before choosing that route.
