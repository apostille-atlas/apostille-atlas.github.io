A divorce decree (or dissolution judgment) is apostilled by the state where the divorce was granted, on a certified copy from the court or vital-records office that keeps the record. It is not a document you prepare by having a notary stamp a photocopy. Texas lists divorce decrees as recordable documents a notary cannot notarize, and Georgia's GSCCCA has a dedicated FAQ for divorce-decree apostilles.

Do I need to notarize a divorce decree before the apostille?

For the states that publish a clear rule, the answer is no: you need the court's (or vital-records office's) certified copy. Texas's authentication page puts divorce decrees with marriage licenses, judgments and birth certificates among recordable documents that "cannot be notarized or turned into certified copies by a notary public" and must be less than five years old. Georgia asks for "an official certified copy issued by the county Clerk of Superior Court." Florida's accepted-documents list includes "Documents filed in a court proceeding" and "Documents recorded by the Clerk of the Court" among the public records a Florida notary cannot attest as copies. If someone tells you to notarize the decree itself, check that state's official page first.

What if I divorced in one state and live in another?

Use the state where the divorce was granted. Washington's Secretary of State lists divorce certificates among vital records that must be apostilled by the issuing state, and New Jersey says it cannot apostille other states' vital records. Most offices accept requests by mail; see getting an apostille while living abroad if you are outside the U.S.

Do I need a translation too?

That depends on the destination country, so check its consulate; our apostille and certified translation guide shows what Spain, Italy and Germany ask for.
