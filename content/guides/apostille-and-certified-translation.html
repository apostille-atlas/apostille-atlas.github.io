An apostille only confirms who signed a U.S. public document; it says nothing about what the document says. So when a foreign office needs to read your birth certificate, FBI check or diploma, it may also ask for a translation, and the rules for that come from the destination country, not from the U.S. office that issues the apostille. This guide sets out what the official pages say, so you can tell whether you need a translation, which kind, and whether it goes before or after the apostille.

Does the apostille itself need to be translated?

No. The Hague Conference's Apostille Handbook says an apostille may not be rejected because it is drawn up in a language other than the destination's, and that no further formality, including translation, may be required for the apostille itself. It adds that the destination can still reject the underlying document if it is not in its official language and has no translation (HCCH Apostille Handbook, paras 304 to 305). The Spanish consulate in Boston puts it the same way: "The translation does not need an Apostille and the Apostille does not need a translation" (Consulate of Spain in Boston).

Should the translation come before or after the apostille?

There are two routes, and the receiving office decides which one it accepts:

  1. Apostille the original, then translate. You get the apostille on the U.S. document, then a translator recognized by the destination translates it. This is what Spain and Germany describe below.
  2. Apostille a notarized translation. A plain translation is not a public document, but the Apostille Handbook says the translator can swear an affidavit about its accuracy before a notary; the notarial certificate is then apostilled and travels with the translation (HCCH Apostille Handbook, para 187). Use this route only when the destination asks for an apostilled translation.

If the receiving office does not say, ask it before you pay for either step. You may also need the U.S. original apostilled first: for which office does that, see state or federal: which office?

How do I get a translation apostilled in the U.S.?

A translation is apostilled through a notary: the translator signs a statement before a notary, and the state where the notary is commissioned apostilles the notarization. What the state offices publish:

GeorgiaIf the destination requests apostilles on translations, the translator must sign an affidavit, before a Georgia notary, that they know English and the other language and that the translation is true and complete. The notary cannot also be the translator. (GSCCCA)$3 per apostille

North CarolinaObtain a certified or notarized English translation if the document is in a language other than English. (NC SOS)$10 per document

Fees are the state apostille fee only, from each state's page in our directory; the notary and the translator charge their own fees, and none of these offices publishes a translation price. All state fees side by side are in apostille cost by state.

Does a document in another language need an English translation before a U.S. state will apostille it?

It depends on the state. Georgia says no: "The language of the document is not relevant," because the apostille authenticates the notary's signature and seal (GSCCCA). North Carolina tells you to get a certified or notarized English translation (NC SOS), and New Jersey recommends one (NJ DORES). Check your state's page in the directory before mailing anything.

How much does a certified translation cost?

None of the official pages above sets a translation price; it is set by the translator. The government cost on the U.S. side is the apostille fee, and New Jersey charges it twice when a notarized translation is attached to a vital record.
