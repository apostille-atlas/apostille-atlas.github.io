If you are moving to Canada, studying or working there, or sending a U.S. birth certificate, diploma, power of attorney or FBI background check to a Canadian office, you may have heard that Canada now uses apostilles. That is true, but it does not mean every U.S. document needs one. The Apostille Convention entered into force for Canada on January 11, 2024, after Canada deposited its instrument of accession on May 12, 2023 (HCCH; Global Affairs Canada glossary).

Do U.S. documents need an apostille to be used in Canada?

Not automatically. Global Affairs Canada says: "Under Canadian law, you do not have to get foreign public documents authenticated before using them in Canada." It adds that "some individuals or institutions in Canada may still only accept foreign documents that have been authenticated" (Global Affairs Canada). So the deciding question is what the receiving office (an immigration program, a court, a licensing body, a school, an employer) asks for. If its instructions do not mention authentication, an apostille, or legalization, the official page gives no reason to get one.

If a Canadian office asks for authentication, what do I get?

An apostille from the U.S., because the United States is a party to the Convention. Global Affairs Canada says a document issued in a Convention country should get an apostille "from the competent authority in that country," and that Global Affairs Canada itself can only authenticate Canadian documents, not foreign ones (Global Affairs Canada). The U.S. competent authorities include the U.S. Department of State's Office of Authentications for federal agency documents, clerks of the federal courts for their own court documents, and the authorities of each state for that state's public documents (HCCH). If the Canadian office also asks for authentication from Global Affairs Canada, the department asks you to email it the proof with an explanation at docs@international.gc.ca (Global Affairs Canada).

Which U.S. office apostilles my document?

It depends on who issued or notarized it:

  • State and local documents (birth, marriage and death certificates, court records, diplomas and other notarized documents): the apostille office of the state where the document was issued or notarized. See the official fee, address and method on our state pages for New York, California, Texas, Michigan, Washington and the other states in the directory. Starting with a vital record? Read the birth certificate apostille guide.
  • Federal documents such as an FBI background check: the U.S. Department of State, which charges $20 per document with Form DS-4194 and processes mailed requests within 5 weeks of receipt (U.S. Department of State; details on our Department of State guide and the FBI background check apostille guide).

Do U.S. documents for Canada still need Canadian consulate legalization?

The Global Affairs Canada pages we checked do not describe any legalization step at a Canadian embassy or consulate for U.S. documents. They say foreign documents need no authentication under Canadian law, and that when authentication is requested, a Convention country's document gets an apostille from that country (Global Affairs Canada). Under the Convention, the apostille replaces legalization (Global Affairs Canada glossary).

Does my document need a translation for Canadian immigration?

Only if it is not in English or French. Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada (IRCC) says that unless it tells you otherwise, supporting documents must be in English or French, and any other document must come with the English or French translation, an affidavit from the translator and a certified photocopy of the original (IRCC Help Centre). U.S. public documents are usually in English already. More on order and timing: apostille plus certified translation.

What about Canadian documents going to the U.S. or elsewhere?

That runs the other way. Alberta, British Columbia, Ontario, Quebec and Saskatchewan issue apostilles for documents issued in their provinces; Global Affairs Canada handles Government of Canada documents and documents issued or notarized in Manitoba, New Brunswick, Newfoundland and Labrador, the Northwest Territories, Nova Scotia, Nunavut, Prince Edward Island and Yukon (Global Affairs Canada). Global Affairs Canada says the apostilles it issues do not expire, but older documents with its pre-2024 red authentication stamp must be resubmitted for an apostille if they are going to a Convention country (Global Affairs Canada).
