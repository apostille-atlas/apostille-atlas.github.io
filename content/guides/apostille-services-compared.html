Every apostille comes from a government office: the state that issued or notarized your document, or the U.S. Department of State for federal documents. A private service does not issue apostilles. It files your document with that same office for you, for a fee. This page compares the two routes using the official fees from our state pages and the prices services publish on their own websites. Not sure which office handles your document? Start with state or federal: which office?

When is doing it yourself the better choice?

  • You are in the U.S. and not in a hurry. You send the same document to the same office a service would, and save at least $95 to $118 per document compared with the lowest published service price (see the table above).
  • Your state offers in-person service. 10 of the 11 states we cover list walk-in or in-person service. California, for example, offers same-day service at its Sacramento and Los Angeles offices. Check your state in the directory.
  • You have one or two documents from one office. The paperwork is usually a cover sheet or form, the fee and a return envelope. Each state page lists exactly what to send.

When is a paid service worth it?

  • You live abroad. Mailing to a U.S. office from abroad and arranging return shipping is harder. The services above publish international return options.
  • You have a close deadline on a federal document. The U.S. Department of State says it processes mailed requests within 5 weeks of receipt, and walk-in drop-offs in Washington, DC within 7 business days. A service that can drop documents off in person may be faster than mailing them yourself. Fingerprints & Apostilles, for example, says it walks documents into the Department of State. See FBI background check apostille.
  • You also need a translation. Apostille USA lists certified translation at $199 for up to five pages, and Apostille Depot offers translation as a priced add-on. Ask the receiving office whether the translation should come before or after the apostille.
  • Your documents come from several offices. Each state only apostilles its own documents, and federal documents go to the Department of State, so a birth certificate and an FBI check mean two separate requests. A service can handle both.

What should I check before paying a service?

  1. Is the government fee included? Some prices include it and some don't say.
  2. What does shipping cost, both ways? Some prices include return shipping and some add it.
  3. Is the price per document? Each document needs its own apostille, so three documents cost three times as much.
  4. What happens if the office rejects the document? Apostille Depot, for example, says fees are non-refundable once an order has been submitted to a government office.
  5. Does your destination country accept apostilles? If not, you need legalization instead. Check which countries accept apostilles first.
