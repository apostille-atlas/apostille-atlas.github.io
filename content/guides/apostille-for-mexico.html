If you are moving to Mexico, applying for temporary or permanent residency, registering a marriage there, or registering a U.S.-born child as a Mexican national, you will run into the word apostilla. Mexico has been a party to the Apostille Convention since August 14, 1995 (accession deposited December 1, 1994), so a U.S. apostille is all the authentication a U.S. public document needs; there is no embassy legalization step (HCCH status table). Whether you need one at all depends on where you file: at a Mexican consulate in the U.S., or with an office inside Mexico.

Do U.S. documents need an apostille for a Mexican visa at a consulate in the U.S.?

Usually not, according to the consulate requirement sheets we checked. The Consulate General of Mexico in San Diego's 2026 sheet for the temporary resident visa (family unit) says documents other than the passport that were issued by a country other than the United States or Mexico must carry an apostille or legalization and an official Spanish translation (Consulate of Mexico in San Diego). The Consulate of Mexico in Orlando says the same for documents that "originate from outside of the United States and Mexico," and asks for a Spanish translation by a certified translator only if a marriage certificate is not in English or Spanish (Consulate of Mexico in Orlando). San Diego adds that documents issued outside its consular district may be sent for verification or that additional documents may be requested (San Diego). Requirements are set per consulate, so check the sheet published by the consulate where you have your appointment.

When do U.S. documents need an apostille inside Mexico?

When you file them with a Mexican authority in Mexico. Mexico's National Migration Institute (INM) rules say foreign public documents, except the passport or travel document, must be apostilled or legalized to be valid in Mexico, and list:

  • birth certificates (actas de nacimiento);
  • marriage certificates (actas de matrimonio);
  • other civil status or name documents;
  • public diplomas, certificates, titles and similar records;
  • documents proving guardianship, parental authority or custody.

Documents in other languages must come with a Spanish translation by an officially recognized translator (perito) (INM Lineamientos, Art. 9). INM's requirement sheets for changes of immigration status repeat the rule (INM: cambios de condición), including the change to permanent residency by family unit, where you prove the tie with birth or marriage certificates (INM: residente permanente por unidad familiar).

Mexican civil registries apply the same rule when you register a U.S. birth or marriage in Mexico (inscripción). Colima asks for the foreign birth or marriage certificate apostilled or legalized, plus a translation of the certificate and of the apostille by an authorized translator (Colima); Coahuila asks for the original foreign certificate, its apostille and a Spanish translation by a translator registered with the state's Superior Court of Justice (Coahuila). Tijuana requires an apostille and a translation by a translator authorized by Baja California's Superior Court, but says California apostilles obtained after November 2011 do not need translation (Tijuana). Fees differ by state: Colima lists MXN $411 to authorize the inscription and Coahuila MXN $1,500 (Colima; Coahuila).

Does a U.S.-born child need an apostilled birth certificate for Mexican nationality?

Not if you register the birth at a Mexican consulate in the U.S. The Consulate General of Mexico in Atlanta asks for the long-form U.S. birth certificate (place and time of birth, parents' names and nationalities), a Mexican parent's birth certificate and ID; only people born in a country other than the U.S. need an apostilled or legalized, translated foreign birth certificate. Atlanta says the registration is free and the first certified copy of the Mexican record is free (Consulate of Mexico in Atlanta). If you instead register the birth at a civil registry in Mexico (inscripción), the same consulate says you need the U.S. birth certificate apostilled and translated into Spanish by a certified translator, and that requirements vary by Mexican state (Atlanta).

Which U.S. office apostilles my document for Mexico?

The office in the U.S. jurisdiction that issued it; Mexican offices do not apostille U.S. documents (HCCH):

  • Birth, marriage, divorce and death certificates, state court records, diplomas and notarized documents: the apostille office of the state that issued or notarized them, generally the Secretary of State. For a birth certificate that is the state where you were born. See the official fee, address and method on our pages for California, Texas, Arizona, Illinois, New York, Florida and the rest of the directory. Document-specific steps: birth certificate, marriage certificate, divorce decree and diploma and transcript apostilles.
  • Federal documents such as an FBI background check: the U.S. Department of State's Office of Authentications, which charges $20 per document (HCCH) and processes mailed requests within 5 weeks of receipt (U.S. Department of State; see our Department of State guide). The Mexican government pages we checked for residency do not list an FBI background check as a requirement.

What should I do first: translation or apostille?

Apostille first, then translate in Mexico if the office asks. The registry pages above ask for a translation of the apostilled document, and Colima's asks for the apostille itself to be translated too (Colima). Because the translator usually has to be one authorized by the court or registry of the Mexican state where you file (Coahuila; Tijuana), check that office's list before paying for a translation in the U.S. None of the official pages we checked sets a maximum age for the certificate itself, except Colima, which asks for certified records no more than 5 years old (Colima).
