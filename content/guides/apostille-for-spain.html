Moving to Spain to study, teach or work remotely usually means apostilling an FBI background check, and often a birth certificate or a notarized letter too. Spain has been a party to the Hague Apostille Convention since September 25, 1978 (HCCH), so an apostille is all the U.S. side needs. Here is what the Spanish consulates in the U.S. publish, and how it fits with the apostille process. Rules differ a little by consulate, so check the page of the consulate that serves your state.

Which documents need an apostille for a Spanish visa?

  • FBI background check (student visas for stays over 180 days, applicants 18 and older). It must be the FBI Identity History Summary; "Local Police or State Police background checks will not be accepted" (Consulate in New York). San Francisco and Boston say it must be based on fingerprints.
  • Police records from other countries where you lived during the past 5 years, apostilled (or legalized, for non-Hague countries) and translated into Spanish.
  • Sponsorship documents, if your parents or guardians pay for your studies: the New York consulate says both the notarized sponsorship letter and the birth certificate must be apostilled or legalized and translated by a sworn translator.
  • Minors' birth certificates: the Chicago consulate asks for the original birth certificate of an applicant under 18, apostilled and translated into Spanish (Consulate in Chicago).

A birth certificate or a notarized letter gets a state apostille from the state where it was issued or notarized; see our birth certificate guide and state or federal: which office?

Does the FBI check need a state or a federal apostille?

Federal. The Chicago consulate says the FBI check must be "legalized with the Federal Apostille of the Hague Convention requested to the Department of State in Washington DC" and that a "Local State apostille is not valid." San Francisco says the same (Consulate in San Francisco). That apostille comes from the U.S. Department of State, not your state; our FBI background check apostille guide walks through the steps.

How long does the whole process take?

  1. FBI check: the FBI says online requests come back within 48 hours of electronic fingerprinting at a participating Post Office, or about 10 days after it receives a mailed fingerprint card; the fee is $18 (FBI).
  2. Federal apostille: within 5 weeks of receipt by mail, or within 7 business days for a walk-in drop-off at 600 19th Street NW, Washington, DC; $20 per document (U.S. Department of State). Our fastest FBI check apostille guide compares the routes.
  3. Sworn translation into Spanish. The Boston consulate says "the translation does not need an Apostille and the Apostille does not need a translation." No official turnaround is published.
  4. Visa: apply between 6 months and 2 months before your program starts (New York, Chicago). Allow at least 5 weeks after a complete application in New York, at least 4 weeks in Chicago, and 5 to 8 weeks in San Francisco, according to each consulate.

Which Spanish consulate do I apply to?

The one that covers where you live or study. The New York consulate serves New York, Connecticut, Pennsylvania, New Jersey and Delaware. Chicago serves Illinois, Indiana, Iowa, Kansas, Kentucky, Michigan, Minnesota, Missouri, Nebraska, North Dakota, Ohio, South Dakota and Wisconsin. San Francisco serves Northern California, Nevada, Oregon, Idaho, Wyoming, Montana, Washington, Alaska and Hawaii. Other states are covered by Spain's other U.S. consulates; check their own pages. Applications go through the BLS visa center the consulate names.

What gets an apostille rejected for Spain?

  • A state apostille on an FBI check (Chicago, San Francisco).
  • A state or local police check instead of the FBI's (all four consulates above).
  • A check older than the consulate's 6-month limit when you apply (Chicago, San Francisco) or arrive (Boston).
