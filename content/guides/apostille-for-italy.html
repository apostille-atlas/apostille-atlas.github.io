Applying for Italian citizenship by descent (iure sanguinis) from the U.S. means collecting a chain of American vital records, apostilling each one in the right office and translating most of them into Italian. Italy has been a party to the Hague Apostille Convention since February 11, 1978 (HCCH), so an apostille is all the U.S. side needs. Here is what the Italian consulates in New York and San Francisco publish, and which U.S. office apostilles each document. Rules differ by consulate, so read the page of the one that serves your state.

Who can still apply after the 2025 law change?

Decree-Law 36/2025, converted into Law 74/2025, changed the rules. Under the new Article 3-bis of Law 91/1992, a person born abroad who holds another citizenship is considered never to have acquired Italian citizenship, unless an exception applies (Consulate in New York, Consulate in San Francisco). The consulates list these exceptions:

  • the application (administrative or court) was filed by 11:59 PM Rome time on March 27, 2025, or was based on an appointment confirmed by that time;
  • a parent or grandparent holds, or held at death, only Italian citizenship;
  • an Italian parent (including adoptive) lived in Italy for at least two consecutive years after becoming a citizen and before the applicant's birth or adoption.

The consulates do not assess eligibility in advance. Check that your case fits before you pay for records and apostilles.

The Italian ancestor's own birth and marriage records come from the Italian comune, issued within the last six months, and need no U.S. apostille.

State or federal apostille: which office do I use?

A state-issued record or a document notarized in a state gets a state apostille from that state, as the New York consulate's apostille page says. A federal record, such as a USCIS or NARA letter, gets the federal apostille from the U.S. Department of State: $20 per document, processed within 5 weeks of receipt by mail (U.S. Department of State). If your documents come from several states, each state apostilles its own; see state or federal: which office? and our birth certificate guide.

What do the main states require first?

  • New York: $10 per document. Locally issued birth, death and marriage certificates and all notarized documents must be certified by the County Clerk first; for New York City birth and death certificates, ask for a Letter of Exemplification when ordering (NY Department of State).
  • New Jersey: $25 per certificate. Attaching a notarized translation to a vital record means two apostilles and two fees (NJ DORES).
  • Florida: $10 per document, or $20 if certified by a county Clerk of the Court; mail only (Florida Department of State).
  • California and other states: see each page in the apostille directory.

How much does the citizenship application cost, and how long does it take?

Both consulates charge a €600 fee per adult application for recognition by descent, paid in U.S. dollars by money order at the quarter's exchange rate, and not refunded if the application is denied (New York, San Francisco). San Francisco says complete applications are processed within 24 months; New York says applications are processed "within the time limits established by law." Apostille fees and translations are extra.

How do I submit the application?

Book a virtual appointment on the Prenot@mi portal and confirm it between 10 and 3 days before the date. On the appointment day, do not go to the consulate: mail the full application that day (New York asks for certified mail), with your name, the appointment date and the Prenot@mi booking code on the envelope. A package mailed before the appointment date is returned at your expense, and documents are kept whatever the outcome (New York, San Francisco). New York serves New York, Connecticut, part of New Jersey and Bermuda; San Francisco serves Northern and Central California, Alaska, Hawaii, Idaho, Montana, Oregon, Utah, Washington and some Pacific territories. Other states are served by Italy's other U.S. consulates.

What gets an Italian citizenship application rejected?

  • A missing document: both consulates reject incomplete applications, and you must book again and pay the fee again.
  • Forms notarized outside your state of residence, or dated more than six months before the appointment (New York).
  • A missing apostille or translation on a U.S. vital record.
  • Mailing the package before the appointment date.

If you live outside the U.S. while gathering records, see getting an apostille while living abroad. If you would rather not file each state apostille yourself, files state apostilles for a fee; doing it yourself with each state office costs only the official fee.
